Plain text is eternal; delivery formats come and go

Every writer has a file like this: notes in a .txt, a draft in a bare editor, minutes in a notes app, a manuscript exported from a distraction-free writing tool. Plain text is the most durable format ever invented — readable on every machine since the seventies — but the world's inboxes, portals and printers ask for PDF. PlainPDF is the deliberate, typeset bridge between the two: not a screenshot of your text, not a browser print dialog's improvisation, but a properly paginated document whose margins, font and rhythm you chose.

The difference shows in the details print dialogs get wrong. Text is wrapped by measuring real font metrics, so lines break cleanly at your margins rather than at a window's width. Paragraphs breathe with consistent spacing. An optional header line repeats on every page like a proper document, and page numbers sit centred at the foot — the two touches that separate a document from a dump.

Choosing a font like it matters — because it does

Three classic faces cover nearly every honest job. Times gives prose the bookish authority of print: essays, manuscripts, formal letters and anything that will be read at length on paper. Helvetica is the neutral modern voice — reports, CV-adjacent documents, instructions — clean at every size and invisible in the best way. Courier is the specialist: screenplays and code-adjacent text by tradition, and anywhere character alignment must be exact, since every letter takes the same width.

Size and margin are a pair. Twelve-point Times at comfortable margins is the manuscript standard for a reason — editors' eyes are calibrated to it. Ten to eleven points suit dense reference text on A4; fourteen and up serve large-print needs and presentation handouts. Wider margins rest the eye and leave room for annotation; narrower margins pack a long document into fewer sheets. PlainPDF exposes exactly these levers and no clutter beyond them.

How to turn text into a PDF, step by step

Paste your text, or load a .txt file directly — the word and character counts confirm what arrived. Blank lines become paragraph breaks; single line breaks are preserved as line breaks, which keeps verse, addresses and lists shaped as you wrote them. Set font, size, page and margin, add a header if the document will live in a binder or a shared folder — a title or a date in small grey type at the top of every page is quietly invaluable there.

Tick page numbers for anything longer than two pages, press Build, and download. The result is a true text PDF: selectable, searchable, tiny in file size, and identical on every machine — the entire point of the format. If the layout displeases, adjust a lever and rebuild; your text is untouched in the box, and iteration costs seconds because the typesetting happens on your own device.

The jobs hiding in plain text

Writers submit manuscripts and contest entries formatted to a house spec from raw drafts. Administrators turn meeting minutes and circulars into distributable documents. Lawyers' clerks, teachers and trainers convert plain-text originals — often generated by other software, dictation tools or AI assistants — into handouts and filings. Developers generate logs, readmes and documentation as PDFs for clients who will never open a markdown file. In each case the source is humble text and the destination demands presentable PDF; the gap between them is pure typesetting.

There is a longer game too: PDF is an archiving format. Converting the plain-text records of a project, a correspondence or a journal into paginated PDFs with headers and page numbers creates artefacts that read intentionally decades later, when the notes app is gone and the .txt's context with it. A header line carrying a title and date is a gift to whoever opens the file in twenty years — quite possibly you.

What plain text cannot express — said plainly

Plain text carries no bold, no italics, no headings, no tables — and PlainPDF does not pretend otherwise. Emphasis you need must live in the prose itself, or the document belongs in a richer editor first. What the format does carry — words, in order, with paragraph and line structure — is preserved completely and typeset with care. For the enormous class of documents that are exactly that, the honesty is a feature: no surprise styles, no formatting archaeology, no wondering what a pasted block dragged in with it.

Unicode deserves a footnote: the built-in PDF fonts cover Latin scripts handsomely, including accents, while characters far outside those ranges may not render in the standard faces. For English and European-language text — the overwhelming majority of plain-text files — output is flawless. Check page one of your download if your text mixes scripts, as you would with any typesetting you care about.

Privacy: your words are the whole payload

Notes, drafts and minutes are unpublished by definition — often the most candid text a person produces. PlainPDF typesets them inside your browser tab; the text exists only in the page you are looking at and in the file you download. There is no upload, no server-side document history, no account holding your drafts. Close the tab and the tool forgets everything, because it never knew anything anywhere else.

That is the right architecture for a converter whose input is, literally, your words. It also makes the tool dependable at the worst connectivity moments — once loaded, it typesets happily offline, on a train, in a cabin, at a client's site — turning text into presentable documents wherever the words happen to be.

Typesetting habits that make text feel published

Let paragraphs do the formatting: one idea per paragraph, a blank line between them, and no manual indentation — the consistent rhythm reads as professional without a single style command. Keep line length humane by respecting margins; the classic measure of forty-five to seventy-five characters exists because eyes lose their place on longer journeys back to the margin. If your text contains lists, give each item its own line and a simple marker — the preserved line breaks will hold the shape. Add the header once the document has a name worth repeating: a title and a date turn pages into a document. Finally, proofread the PDF, not the text box — line breaks move meaning, and the page is where your reader will meet every orphan and awkward break.

Frequently Asked Questions

How does PlainPDF handle paragraphs?

Blank lines start new paragraphs with comfortable spacing; single line breaks are kept as line breaks, so verse, addresses and list-like text keep their shape.

Which font should I choose?

Times for formal prose and manuscripts, Helvetica for clean modern documents and reports, Courier for screenplays, code-flavoured text or exact character alignment.

Can I add a header and page numbers?

Yes — an optional header line prints in small grey type at the top of every page, and page numbers ('3 / 12') can be centred at the foot. Both are set before building.

Is the output text selectable?

Yes. PlainPDF typesets real text with embedded standard fonts — the PDF is searchable, selectable and tiny, unlike image-based conversions.

Can I load a .txt file instead of pasting?

Yes — the Load button reads a .txt file straight into the editor, with word and character counts shown, ready to typeset.

Is my text uploaded anywhere?

Never. Typesetting happens entirely in your browser; your words exist only in your tab and in the PDF you download.
